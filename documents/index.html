Promotion Cannibalization

Did the promotion create new demand, or just move existing sales from one of our own products to another?

The Problem Statement

Four questions that frame the challenge.

1

What is happening?

During major promotions, sales of a promoted SKU increase significantly, but sales of other SKUs within the same brand/category decline at the same time. The business cannot reliably distinguish genuine incremental demand from customers simply switching between its own products.

2

What is impacted?

  • Promotion ROI
  • Brand revenue
  • SKU mix
  • Retailer margins
  • Decisions on which products to promote
3

Why is it a challenge?

Promotion performance is typically evaluated using SKU-level sales uplift, while substitution between pack sizes, variants, and competing SKUs is difficult to attribute across retailers, regions and channels.

4

Why is it important?

A promotion that appears to generate ₹10Cr in incremental sales could actually be shifting a substantial portion of existing demand from higher-margin products. Misjudging this can lead to millions of rupees being spent on ineffective promotions.

Business Relevance

Why this matters at scale.

Promotions drive CPG sales

An increase in promoted SKU sales doesn't necessarily mean incremental revenue for the brand. If consumers simply switch from other SKUs in the same portfolio, the company shifts existing demand rather than creating new demand.

Consequences

  • Misleading promotion ROI
  • Reduced margins
  • Inefficient marketing spend
  • Poor decisions on what to promote

Leakage at scale

Even a small degree of cannibalization across high-volume promotions can translate into significant revenue and margin leakage.

The core question: Is a promotion truly creating incremental sales, or simply moving sales from one SKU to another?
